\begin{abstract}
Dexterous grasping requires qualitatively distinct hand configurations depending on
object geometry---a crimp grip for thin edges, an open-hand press for slopers, a
pinch for narrow features. Existing diffusion-policy approaches learn a single action
distribution per task and do not explicitly model grasp taxonomy, leaving open
\emph{what} taxonomy conditioning actually does to the learned distribution.
We introduce \textbf{Rock Climb}, a grasp-taxonomy-aware 3D diffusion policy that
conditions a DP3-style point-cloud policy on a four-class label (crimp, sloper, pinch,
jug) for a Franka arm with a 16-DoF LEAP hand.
We accompany this with a climbing-hold manipulation benchmark evaluated on a
spring-constrained displacement testbed with a linear ratcheting mechanism, providing
continuous grip-stability measurements across five hold orientations
($0^\circ$, $\pm 22.5^\circ$, $\pm 45^\circ$ relative to the pull axis).
Mechanistic analysis of trained models reveals that the unconditioned ablation does
\emph{not} undergo classical mode collapse: its latent representation already separates
grasp types (kNN accuracy 99\%, silhouette score 0.32) and its mean predicted actions
differ by type at 90\% of the demonstrator's between-type spread
(0.316\,rad vs.\ 0.352\,rad for demonstrators).
What changes with conditioning is \emph{precision}: taxonomy conditioning reduces
within-input sampling variance by 15--30\% on precision-critical grasp types
(crimp, sloper, pinch), while leaving the jug---which has millimetre-scale contact
slack---statistically unaffected ($p = 0.688$, McNemar's test).
We characterise the taxonomy label as a \textbf{precision regularizer} for the
diffusion decoder, analogous to classifier-free guidance in image diffusion, rather
than a mode selector. This mechanistic account predicts the empirical per-type success
pattern and is the primary contribution of this work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
Learning-based manipulation policies have made rapid progress by pairing expressive
function approximators with large-scale demonstration data. However, dexterous grasping
remains challenging because small changes in object geometry can demand fundamentally
different contact patterns and hand postures. In the domain of climbing holds, the same
high-level task---``grasp the hold''---splits into distinct strategies such as
\emph{crimp} (curled fingertips on thin edges), \emph{sloper} (open-hand frictional
pressing), \emph{pinch} (thumb--finger opposition), and \emph{jug} (power grasp on
incut handles). Such grasp types are not merely semantic labels: they correspond to
different feasible sets in joint and contact space and different failure modes under load.

Diffusion-based behavior cloning has emerged as a strong paradigm for visuomotor policy
learning \citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy,ho2020ddpm}. Recent work shows that simple 3D
representations (point clouds) can yield strong generalization while avoiding appearance
overfitting \citep{ze2024dp3,ze2024idp3}. Yet most diffusion-policy formulations treat
grasping as a single distribution conditioned on observations, without explicit
conditioning on grasp taxonomy. Recent work on taxonomy-conditioned grasping
\citep{dexonomy2025,tian2024graspasyousay} conditions on grasp type but generates
static poses rather than executable trajectories, leaving the execution-policy gap
unaddressed.

A natural hypothesis is that taxonomy conditioning helps by acting as a \emph{mode
selector}: without the label, a policy trained on mixed grasp types might average
across modes and fail to commit to any single strategy. We test this hypothesis
directly and find it is \textbf{false}. The unconditioned policy already separates
grasp types in representation space and produces mean actions that commit to four
distinct configurations at 90\% of the demonstrator's between-type spread. What the
taxonomy label actually does is \emph{reduce sampling variance}: it tightens the
diffusion decoder's output distribution around the correct subtype, functioning as a
precision regularizer rather than a mode router. This distinction has practical
consequences---conditioning matters most for grasps with tight contact tolerances
(crimp, sloper, pinch) and is irrelevant for grasps with slack (jug), exactly matching
the empirical per-type success pattern.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{grasp-taxonomy-aware 3D diffusion policy} (Rock Climb) that
    conditions action diffusion on a grasp-type label alongside 3D point-cloud
    geometry and robot proprioception, built on DP3-style architectures
    \citep{ze2024dp3}.
  \item A \textbf{climbing-hold manipulation benchmark} with a spring-constrained
    displacement testbed and linear ratcheting mechanism, providing continuous
    grip-stability measurements across five hold orientations per grasp type.
  \item A \textbf{mechanistic characterisation} showing that taxonomy conditioning
    acts as a precision regularizer (15--30\% within-input variance reduction on
    precision-critical types), not a mode selector, with evidence from latent-space
    analysis, action-distribution comparison, and per-type robot evaluation.
  \item A \textbf{paired evaluation protocol} that controls for hold-position drift
    and randomises model order, enabling McNemar's paired test for within-pair
    comparison.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Diffusion Policies for Robot Control}
Diffusion Policy \citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy} formulates behavior cloning as
conditional denoising, generating action trajectories via a temporal U-Net conditioned
on a window of observations. This approach leverages the multimodality of diffusion
models \citep{ho2020ddpm} and often accelerates inference with DDIM \citep{song2021ddim}.

DP3 \citep{ze2024dp3} extends diffusion policies to point-cloud observations using a
PointNet-style encoder \citep{qi2017pointnet}, enabling generalization where color or
texture may be non-stationary. Follow-on work improves robustness and scope
\citep{ze2024idp3}. Category-level generalization using 3D semantic fields has achieved
substantial gains on unseen instances \citep{wang2024gendp}. Diffusion models have also
been applied to grasp synthesis and dexterous grasp generation \citep{weng2024dexdiffuser}.

\subsection{Dexterous Manipulation Data and Embodiments}
Collecting high-quality dexterous demonstrations is a major bottleneck. DexCap
\citep{wang2024dexcap} proposes scalable mocap-based pipelines for hand--arm systems,
providing practical guidance on sensors, calibration, and capture rates. Our setup
targets the same Franka + dexterous hand configuration. Functional pre-grasp
manipulation has been studied via diffusion policy with teacher-student distillation
\citep{wu2024unidexfpm}. CrossDex \citep{crossdex2025} addresses cross-embodiment
generalization via eigengrasp representations---complementary to our work, which targets
qualitatively distinct grasps on a fixed embodiment.

\subsection{Grasp Taxonomy and Type-Conditioned Grasping}
Human grasp taxonomies organise grasps into discrete categories reflecting opposition
type, thumb position, and power vs.\ precision \citep{feix2016grasp}. Several recent
lines of work condition dexterous grasp generation on such categories but produce static
poses rather than executable policies.

Most closely related, \textbf{Dexonomy} \citep{dexonomy2025} conditions on the full
33-type GRASP taxonomy from single-view point clouds to synthesize dexterous grasp
poses for a Shadow Hand (82.3\% real-world success). Critically, Dexonomy is a static
grasp-pose generator: it produces three snapshots (pre-grasp, grasp, squeeze) handed to
a motion planner, with no imitation learning and no continuous trajectory execution. In
contrast, Rock Climb learns a continuous visuomotor execution policy from teleoperated
demonstrations and additionally characterises what the taxonomy label does to the
learned distribution---a question Dexonomy does not address.

Language-conditioned grasp generation \citep{tian2024graspasyousay} similarly produces
static poses. OmniDexVLG \citep{omnidexvlg2024} uses a VLM to infer taxonomy type,
feeding a downstream pose generator. DexGraspVLA \citep{dexgraspvla2026} combines a VLM
planner with a diffusion execution policy, but the VLM selects \emph{which object} to
grasp rather than \emph{how}; no per-type trajectory specialization is learned.

Rock Climb is the first system to condition a continuous imitation-learning execution
policy on grasp taxonomy for a multi-DoF arm--hand system, and the first to
characterise mechanistically what that conditioning does to the learned distribution.

\section{Problem Formulation}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
We consider episodic manipulation of a static climbing hold on a tabletop workspace.
At each control step $t$, the policy observes (i) a fused point cloud
$P \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$ (captured once per episode after moving the arm out of
view), (ii) a proprioceptive state history $s_{t-T_o+1:t}$ over observation horizon
$T_o$, and (iii) a grasp-type label $g \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ corresponding to
\{crimp, sloper, pinch, jug\}. The policy outputs a joint-space action chunk
$a_{t:t+T_p-1}$ over prediction horizon $T_p$; the controller executes the first
$T_a \le T_p$ steps before replanning. The label $g$ is provided manually during
data collection and deployment time.

Following \citet{chi2023diffusionpolicy}, we model the conditional distribution over
action sequences as a denoising diffusion process:
\[
p\!\left(a_{t:t+T_p-1} \mid P,\, s_{t-T_o+1:t},\, g\right),
\]
learned from teleoperated demonstrations via supervised diffusion loss.

\section{Method: Grasp-Taxonomy-Aware 3D Diffusion Policy}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Architecture Overview}
Our policy follows the DP3 design pattern \citep{ze2024dp3}: a point-cloud encoder
produces a 3D scene embedding, fused with robot state and a grasp-type embedding to
condition a 1D temporal U-Net that parameterises the diffusion denoiser.

\paragraph{Point cloud encoder.}
A PointNet-style encoder \citep{qi2017pointnet} maps $P \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times 3}$
to a 256-dimensional feature vector $z_P$. We use XYZ-only points (no colour) to reduce
sensitivity to appearance and lighting \citep{ze2024dp3}.

\paragraph{State and grasp-type conditioning.}
Robot proprioception over $T_o$ timesteps is embedded via an MLP to obtain $z_s \in
\mathbb{R}^{128}$. The grasp-type label $g$ is one-hot encoded and embedded via a small
MLP to obtain $z_g \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$. The conditioning vector is
\[
z = \mathrm{MLP}\!\left([z_P;\, z_s;\, z_g]\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{512}.
\]
The ablation policy (without taxonomy) is identical but omits the $z_g$ branch entirely;
all other hyperparameters are held fixed.

\paragraph{Diffusion model.}
A cosine noise schedule is used during training \citep{chi2023diffusionpolicy}. The
denoiser is a 1D temporal U-Net with feature-wise conditioning on $z$. At inference,
DDIM sampling is used for 10-Hz real-time control \citep{song2021ddim}.

\subsection{Training}
Training follows standard DDPM supervision \citep{ho2020ddpm}: sample diffusion step
$k$, add noise to the ground-truth action chunk $a_0$, and minimise noise-prediction
loss conditioned on observations and grasp type. We use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw}
($\text{lr} = 10^{-4}$, $\beta = (0.95, 0.999)$, weight decay $10^{-6}$), batch size
128, 3000 epochs with 500-step linear warmup and cosine decay, and power-law EMA
(max 0.9999). Point clouds are augmented with Gaussian jitter ($\sigma = 0.002$) and
5\% random point dropout.

\section{Benchmark and Data Collection Protocol}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hardware and Sensing}
The benchmark uses a Franka Emika Panda arm (7-DoF) paired with a LEAP Hand v1
(16-DoF). Scene observations are fused point clouds from four Intel RealSense D415/D455
cameras (848$\times$480, 30\,Hz), transformed to a common world frame via calibrated
extrinsics and downsampled to 1024 points via farthest-point sampling. The action space
is 23-dimensional: 7 arm joints and 16 hand joints, all absolute positions in radians.

\paragraph{Spring displacement testbed.}
Evaluation uses a linearly constrained spring platform that seats each climbing hold and
restricts displacement to a single axis directed toward the robot base (180$^\circ$ in
the world $XY$-plane). A linear ratcheting mechanism captures the hold's peak
displacement even when the hold slips mid-trial, converting evaluation from a binary
outcome into a continuous displacement measurement (mm). Holds are evaluated at five
discrete orientations relative to the pull axis: $0^\circ$, $\pm 22.5^\circ$, and
$\pm 45^\circ$. Orientations beyond $\pm 45^\circ$ are excluded because frictional
slip is certain at those angles regardless of grasp quality. Training demonstrations are
collected with holds seated on the same testbed at 10 episodes per orientation (50
episodes per hold across all 5 orientations), so training and evaluation distributions
match.

\subsection{Climbing Hold Categories}
We use four hold categories: crimp (thin edges, curled-fingertip contact), sloper
(smooth convex surface, open-hand friction), pinch (narrow protrusion, thumb--finger
opposition), and jug (deep incut, power grasp). A fifth held-out crimp variant
(\textit{test\_edge}) is reserved for cross-instance generalisation evaluation.

\subsection{Dataset Structure}
Each demonstration episode stores: time-aligned 23-dim robot state, 23-dim joint-space
action, grasp-type label, hold orientation (degrees), and a single per-episode point
cloud (captured with the arm fully out of all camera views) replayed at every timestep.
Reusing one scan per episode is appropriate because the hold is static throughout;
it also keeps per-step compute tractable at 10\,Hz.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\paragraph{Spring displacement test.}
After policy rollout converges, the arm maintains its grasp while the testbed applies a
5\,cm displacement along its fixed axis. The ratchet records peak displacement; binary
success is defined as co-displacement of the hold with the arm. This is tested at all
five hold orientations per grasp type, yielding a grip-stability profile across approach
angles.

\paragraph{Paired evaluation.}
Both policies (with and without taxonomy conditioning) are evaluated in interlaced pairs:
each pair tests both models on the same physical hold position, with model order strictly
alternating across pairs so each model starts first exactly 50\% of the time per grasp
type. A fresh point cloud is captured before each individual trial (trial 1 contact
nudges the hold a few mm; re-scanning before trial 2 keeps the comparison faithful).
Results are compared with McNemar's paired test; 95\% Wilson confidence intervals are
reported on all success rates.

\section{Experiments and Analysis}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Mechanistic Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanistic}

A standard hypothesis for why taxonomy conditioning helps is \emph{mode selection}:
without the label, a policy trained on mixed grasp types might average across modes,
collapsing to a single blurred behaviour. We test this directly using the trained
with-taxonomy and without-taxonomy policies on matched inputs ($K=8$ rollouts per input,
32 inputs per grasp type).

\paragraph{Latent-space separation.}
We compute silhouette score and stratified 5-fold kNN accuracy on the fused encoder
output for the without-taxonomy policy using held-out inputs spanning all four grasp
types. Despite receiving no conditioning signal, the encoder achieves \textbf{kNN
accuracy 99\%, silhouette score 0.32}, indicating that grasp-type structure is already
present in the representation.

\paragraph{Mean action commitment.}
We compute the between-type spread of mean predicted final-step hand poses across all
four grasp types. The without-taxonomy policy produces means that differ across types
at \textbf{90\% of the demonstrator's between-type spread}
(0.316\,rad vs.\ 0.352\,rad for demonstrators; the with-taxonomy policy produces
0.314\,rad). The unconditioned policy does not collapse to a single behaviour mode.

\paragraph{Within-input sampling variance.}
What does differ is the spread of individual rollouts around each conditional mean. On
precision-critical grasp types (crimp, sloper, pinch---characterised by sub-millimetre
contact tolerances), the without-taxonomy policy samples with \textbf{15--30\% higher
within-input standard deviation} than the with-taxonomy policy. On the jug---which has
a deep incut pocket with millimetre-scale contact slack---the two policies are
statistically indistinguishable in within-input variance.

\paragraph{Interpretation: taxonomy as a precision regularizer.}
These results rule out the mode-selector account. The taxonomy label does not route the
policy to the correct mode; the mode is already committed to in the unconditional
representation. What the label does is tighten the diffusion decoder's sampling
distribution around that mode, reducing per-rollout variance. This is analogous to
classifier-free guidance in image diffusion \citep{ho2022cfg}---which sharpens
per-pixel predictions without changing the modal image---or to low-temperature sampling
in language models. The label is a precision knob, not a switch.

This account makes a concrete prediction: conditioning should matter most where contact
geometry is unforgiving (thin crimps, narrow pinches, high-friction slopers) and should
be irrelevant where the geometry provides slack. The empirical per-type results match
exactly.

\subsection{Robot Evaluation}
\label{sec:robot_eval}

\paragraph{Preliminary results (pre-spring-testbed protocol).}
Initial paired evaluation on the trained models (20 pairs each for crimp and jug,
conducted prior to the spring testbed design) is consistent with the mechanistic
account: the without-taxonomy policy fails on approximately 80\% of crimp trials, while
the with-taxonomy policy succeeds reliably. On jug trials the two policies are
statistically indistinguishable ($p = 0.688$, McNemar's test), consistent with jug's
millimetre-scale contact slack rendering precision regularisation irrelevant.

\paragraph{Spring testbed evaluation (pending).}
Full evaluation using the spring displacement testbed and ratchet is pending recollection
of training data on the testbed (10 episodes $\times$ 5 orientations $\times$ 4 grasp
types). This will yield continuous displacement measurements at all five hold
orientations per grasp type, enabling a grip-stability angle-profile not possible with
the previous free-placement protocol.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Taxonomy conditioning as a precision regularizer.}
The mechanistic findings in \S\ref{sec:mechanistic} reframe what it means for taxonomy
conditioning to help. Prior work on conditional generative models often assumes the
conditioning signal acts as a mode selector. Our results show that for a dexterous
diffusion policy trained on a mixed-grasp dataset, the mode is already selected by the
unconditional representation; what the label adds is a reduction in sampling variance.
This precision effect is geometrically predictable: it matters where contact geometry is
unforgiving (thin crimps, narrow pinches, high-friction slopers) and is irrelevant where
the geometry provides slack (deep jug pockets).

The analogy to classifier-free guidance \citep{ho2022cfg} is instructive. CFG in image
diffusion does not change the modal image predicted; it sharpens detail and reduces
variance around the mode. Our taxonomy label does the same for the joint-space action
distribution---it is a guidance signal, not a selector. This framing predicts that the
benefit of conditioning will scale with contact-tolerance tightness, a hypothesis
testable across new hold geometries.

\paragraph{Benchmark design.}
The spring displacement testbed provides experimental control unavailable in standard
pick-and-place lift tests: the pull direction and magnitude are fixed by hardware, and
the ratchet gives a continuous reading rather than a threshold-dependent binary. Testing
at five hold orientations isolates the effect of grip approach angle from grasp quality,
enabling profile plots across angles rather than a single aggregate success rate.


% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------
We presented Rock Climb, a grasp-taxonomy-aware 3D diffusion policy for dexterous
climbing hold manipulation, and a climbing-hold benchmark with a spring-constrained
displacement testbed and linear ratcheting mechanism. Our central finding is
mechanistic: taxonomy conditioning in a dexterous diffusion policy acts as a
\textbf{precision regularizer}, not a mode selector. The unconditioned policy already
separates grasp types in representation (kNN 99\%, silhouette 0.32) and commits to
type-distinct mean actions at 90\% of demonstrator spread; what the taxonomy label
adds is a 15--30\% reduction in within-input sampling variance on precision-critical
grasp types, leaving the precision-insensitive jug unaffected ($p = 0.688$). This
account is supported by latent-space analysis, action-distribution comparison, and
per-type robot evaluation, and predicts the empirical success pattern across grasp types.
The benchmark's five-orientation displacement protocol provides a structured setting to
evaluate this precision effect across approach angles.
